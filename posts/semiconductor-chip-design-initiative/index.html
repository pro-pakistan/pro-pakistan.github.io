Unlocking Silicon: Pakistan's RISC-V Gambit for Open-Source Chip Sovereignty

Pakistani universities are spearheading a quiet revolution, designing sub-28nm chips using RISC-V to sidestep exorbitant proprietary IP costs. This strategic shift promises to foster local innovation and build resilience against global supply chain disruptions.

Zeeshan Ahmed Senior Tech & Telecom Editor
• 23 Sep 2026
PKR 350 Million ANNUAL IP SAVINGS

From the bustling lanes of Lahore's Hafeez Center, where every second shop sells imported circuit boards and mobile phone components, to the gleaming corporate towers lining Karachi's I.I. Chundrigar Road, the pervasive reliance on foreign technology is undeniable. Pakistan's electronics sector, despite its vibrant entrepreneurial spirit, remains largely an assembly and distribution hub, heavily dependent on imported intellectual property (IP). This reliance exacts a heavy toll: not only in foreign exchange — a constant concern highlighted by SBP circulars on import curbs — but also in the stifling of indigenous design capabilities. The economic burden is palpable, with FBR customs valuation rulings on high-tech imports often reflecting the significant, embedded cost of proprietary chip designs that are licensed from global giants.

However, a quiet, yet profound, architectural shift is underway in Pakistan's academic and research institutions, particularly within Islamabad’s burgeoning tech ecosystem. Driven by a desire for technological self-reliance and the urgent need to cut costs, several university research labs are embracing RISC-V, an open-source instruction set architecture (ISA). This isn't merely an academic exercise; it's a strategic pivot. By designing custom silicon based on RISC-V, these institutions are directly challenging the decades-long dominance of proprietary architectures, aiming to bypass millions of rupees in annual IP royalty payments that bleed into the coffers of international corporations.

The focus of these trailblazing efforts is keenly targeted at critical, high-volume applications: automotive and Internet of Things (IoT) devices. Imagine locally designed chips powering everything from smart city sensors to electric vehicle control units, custom-built for Pakistan’s unique needs. Crucially, these labs are not just dabbling; they are successfully 'taping out' sophisticated designs at sub-28 nanometer (nm) process nodes. This achievement places Pakistan squarely at the forefront of advanced chip design capabilities, proving that local talent can compete on a global scale and develop complex silicon without being beholden to costly, licensed blueprints.

This move towards open-source silicon is more than just an academic curiosity; it’s an economic imperative. With the PTA's DIRBS system routinely grappling with the complexities of IMEI registration for imported devices, the prospect of locally designed and manufactured chips offers a pathway to streamlined compliance and enhanced data security. Moreover, leveraging RISC-V architecture opens up unprecedented opportunities for local startups within the Special Technology Zones Authority (STZA) in Islamabad's Blue Area, potentially fostering an ecosystem of innovators capable of creating bespoke solutions for sectors ranging from local banks like HBL and UBL to telecom giants like Jazz and Ufone, reducing their reliance on costly, off-the-shelf foreign hardware.

The Proprietary IP Conundrum: A Drain on Pakistan's Excheqeur

For decades, Pakistan's tech industry has operated within a framework dictated by proprietary intellectual property. Whether it's the core processors in our smartphones, the microcontrollers in our appliances, or the complex System-on-Chips (SoCs) in automotive components, each unit carries an embedded cost — the royalty paid to the IP owner. These costs, often denominated in foreign currency, accumulate into a substantial outflow from the national exchequer, further straining Pakistan's already precarious balance of payments situation, a concern frequently highlighted in SBP circulars regarding import financing. For local manufacturers and assemblers, these IP costs are inescapable, directly impacting the final price of everything from a locally assembled car to a network router used by a domestic ISP like PTCL. This reliance also makes Pakistan vulnerable to supply chain disruptions and geopolitical shifts, as seen during recent global chip shortages that crippled industries worldwide.

Developing a custom chip, historically, has been an prohibitively expensive endeavor, requiring not just immense capital investment but also deep pockets for licensing core IP from dominant players like ARM Holdings. A single license for a high-performance ARM core can run into millions of US dollars, a figure often converted into hundreds of millions of PKR, making it an unfeasible proposition for most Pakistani enterprises. This financial barrier has prevented the emergence of a robust local chip design industry, pushing local talent towards software development rather than hardware innovation. The result is a persistent dependency that limits our strategic autonomy and economic competitiveness in the rapidly evolving global digital landscape, stifling true indigenous innovation and making it harder for local firms to compete with global tech giants who already have these IP costs amortized over massive sales volumes.

RISC-V: The Open-Source Catalyst for Local Chip Design

RISC-V presents a paradigm shift, offering a completely open-source and royalty-free instruction set architecture. This fundamental difference means that Pakistani engineers and researchers can design, implement, and extend custom processors without incurring any licensing fees for the core architecture. This freedom drastically reduces the financial barrier to entry, making advanced chip design accessible to universities and startups that would otherwise be priced out of the market. The ability to customize the ISA allows for highly optimized designs tailored to specific applications, whether it's for energy-efficient IoT sensors reporting NEPRA-tariff-related data, or robust controllers for smart grids, offering performance comparable to proprietary solutions but at a fraction of the cost, fostering innovation from the ground up rather than relying on off-the-shelf components.

Islamabad's research labs, notably those at NUST and FAST-NUCES, have been at the forefront, achieving significant milestones. Their successful tape-outs of sub-28nm automotive and IoT silicon demonstrate not just academic prowess but practical capability. A single tape-out for such advanced processes can cost upwards of PKR 250 million, encompassing design tools, verification, and foundry services, representing a substantial, yet strategic, investment. The true value, however, lies in the experience gained and the local talent developed, laying the groundwork for a future where Pakistan can design its own secure, sovereign silicon, potentially reducing Pakistan's reliance on foreign currency for tech imports and contributing to the digital economy goals of the country. This local expertise is crucial for addressing unique challenges and developing context-specific solutions that are often overlooked by global chip manufacturers.

The Economic & Strategic Imperative: Beyond Cost Savings

The push for RISC-V in Pakistan extends beyond mere cost savings; it is a strategic imperative for national security and economic resilience. By developing indigenous chip design capabilities, Pakistan can mitigate risks associated with reliance on foreign suppliers, particularly in critical sectors like defense, telecommunications, and national data infrastructure. Imagine a scenario where a critical component for Pakistan's 5G rollout, deployed by local telcos like Zong, could be designed and secured within the country, free from potential backdoors or supply chain vulnerabilities. This self-reliance also fosters a robust domestic tech ecosystem, creating high-value jobs and attracting foreign investment into areas like the STZA, transforming Pakistan from a technology consumer to a technology producer.

Furthermore, an open-source approach promotes collaboration and knowledge sharing within the local academic and industrial landscape. Universities can openly share design blocks and collaborate on complex projects, accelerating the learning curve for budding engineers. This collaborative spirit is essential for building a critical mass of experts capable of tackling the immense challenges of modern chip design. The FBR's role in facilitating the import of essential chip design tools, rather than just finished products, will be crucial. This shift in focus, from taxing consumption to incentivizing production, could unlock immense potential, paving the way for Pakistan to carve out a niche in the global semiconductor value chain, driven by secure, efficient, and locally optimized hardware solutions.

Comparative Analysis: Open-Source vs. Proprietary Licensing Models

The economic models underpinning chip design are starkly different between proprietary and open-source architectures. Proprietary models, such as those prevalent with ARM, involve upfront licensing fees, per-unit royalties, and often stringent usage restrictions. For a startup in Pakistan, even getting access to a basic ARM Cortex-M core for an IoT project can involve a six-figure USD license, a prohibitive cost easily exceeding PKR 30 million. This doesn't even account for the ongoing royalties per chip sold, which quickly add up for volume production. This structured cost model creates a significant barrier to entry and slows down innovation for smaller players.

RISC-V, conversely, operates on an open-source model. The instruction set architecture is freely available, and there are no licensing fees or royalties for implementing a RISC-V core. While development still requires investment in design tools, talent, and foundry access (e.g., a multi-project wafer shuttle costing millions of PKR), the fundamental IP cost is zero. This frees up capital that can be reinvested into R&D, talent acquisition, or market expansion. The long-term implications for Pakistan mean fostering an environment where innovation is limited only by imagination and engineering prowess, not by the size of a company's IP budget. This cost structure is particularly appealing for developing low-cost, high-volume devices for local markets, from agricultural sensors to affordable consumer electronics, fostering an ecosystem much like China's, where a multitude of local companies can innovate on a common, open platform.

Executive Takeaways & Key Insights

  • Government policy should actively incentivize RISC-V adoption through R&D grants and tax breaks for startups designing open-source silicon.
  • Universities must enhance practical chip design curricula, partnering with industry to provide hands-on experience and access to design tools and foundry services.
  • Local industry, especially telcos and auto manufacturers, should explore integrating locally designed RISC-V chips for custom IoT and automotive solutions.
  • Investment in a dedicated semiconductor design park within the STZA, offering shared lab facilities and IP resources, could accelerate commercialization.

Frequently Asked Questions

What exactly is RISC-V and why is it important for Pakistan?

RISC-V is an open-source instruction set architecture (ISA), essentially the fundamental language a computer chip understands. Unlike proprietary ISAs like ARM or x86, RISC-V is free to use and modify without licensing fees. For Pakistan, this means local engineers can design custom, cutting-edge chips without the exorbitant costs of proprietary IP, fostering indigenous innovation and reducing reliance on foreign technology for critical applications like IoT and automotive.

How will this impact ordinary Pakistani consumers or businesses?

While not immediately visible, the impact will be profound. Locally designed RISC-V chips can lead to more affordable and secure electronic devices, from smart home gadgets to industrial controls. Businesses can benefit from tailored, cost-effective solutions for logistics, agriculture, or finance. Longer term, it means a stronger local tech industry, creating high-value jobs and reducing Pakistan's foreign exchange burden, contributing to a more stable digital economy.

What are the biggest challenges Pakistan faces in leveraging RISC-V technology?

The primary challenges include securing sufficient investment for advanced chip design tools and foundry access, bridging the gap between academic research and commercialization, and developing a skilled workforce at scale. Additionally, navigating global supply chains for manufacturing and securing access to advanced fabrication facilities remains a significant hurdle. Government support for R&D and clear regulatory frameworks are crucial to overcome these obstacles.
